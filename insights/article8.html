How Long Does It Take to Audit a Frontier Model?

PRIME DOMAIN CAPITAL RESEARCH · 8 MIN READ · NOVEMBER 2026

In March 2026, one person from METR walked into Anthropic's offices and spent three weeks trying to break the company's internal agent monitoring systems. One person, three weeks, a narrow target: the systems that watch what Anthropic's own AI agents do inside the company. He found vulnerabilities nobody at Anthropic knew about. The engagement was a pilot — the first embedded assessment of a frontier AI developer's internal systems — and it worked precisely because it was small and focused. It also took three weeks to examine one subsystem of one company. A full frontier model audit, the kind regulators and enterprise buyers actually want, is a different animal. It runs three to six months on paper, and the paper is optimistic.

The question in the headline has no single answer because an audit is not one job. It is three jobs stacked in sequence — access, evaluation, reporting — and each runs on a different clock. The testing itself, the part buyers imagine when they picture an audit, is the shortest of the three. What sets the schedule is everything around it: negotiating what the evaluators are allowed to see, finding evaluators qualified to see it, and writing a report the lab will let you publish. A buyer who budgets for the testing and forgets the rest will be surprised twice — first by the calendar, then by the invoice.

Access is the schedule

Every published frontier audit starts with a negotiation, and the negotiation is where months disappear. The lab controls the model, the checkpoints, the training data, and the monitoring systems. The evaluator asks for all of them. The lab offers a subset. What follows is a back-and-forth over scope that can run for weeks before a single test is written. The GPT-4 red-team program, still the best-documented frontier audit, took six months of testing — but the access terms were settled before the clock started, and the 50-plus experts OpenAI hired each spent 10 to 40 hours probing the model, most paid around $100 an hour, according to the Financial Times' reporting at the time. The six months was the testing. The negotiation that preceded it is not in the public record.

The access question has a specific shape. A black-box audit — the evaluator gets the finished model through an API and probes it — can start in days. A white-box audit — the evaluator gets model weights, training checkpoints, and internal documentation — takes months to arrange and is rarely granted in full. Between them sits the gray-box audit: API access plus some documentation, some logging, some ability to test variants. Most frontier audits are gray-box, and the exact shade of gray is negotiated per engagement. The UK AI Security Institute, which evaluated more than 30 frontier systems between November 2023 and October 2025, has built its program around negotiated pre-deployment access — and its own reporting notes that the scope of access, not the difficulty of the tests, is what determines how much an evaluation can actually say.

That is why the most important development in audit timelines in 2026 is not a faster test. It is a different access model. On November 16, 2026, OpenAI announced embedded assessments: four independent organizations — Apollo Research, METR, Transluce, and Goodfire — given employee-like access to internal models, training checkpoints, training and evaluation data, monitoring systems, and researchers. Apollo's pilot runs six assessors over four months, covering training, evaluation, and internal use. Transluce's first assessment runs six weeks starting in early December 2026, with a follow-on of about eight weeks planned for early 2027. METR's first phase focuses on OpenAI's largest frontier reinforcement learning run, looking for misaligned behavior that existing monitors might miss. The point of the arrangement is that the evaluators are present while the model is being built, not after it is finished — because some risks cannot be exposed by testing a finished product.

The three clocks

Once access is settled, the audit itself runs on three clocks. The first is the evaluation clock: the time to design tests, run them, and analyze the results. For a frontier model, this is typically four to eight weeks of active testing. The GPT-4 program ran six months, but that was 50 experts working in parallel on a model no one had audited before. A routine evaluation of a model from a known family — same architecture, new weights — can run faster, because the test suite already exists and the evaluators know where to look. METR's March 2026 Anthropic pilot ran three weeks because the target was narrow: one monitoring system, one evaluator, one question. Scale the target to a full model and the clock scales with it.

The second clock is the reporting clock, and it is the one buyers underestimate most. A frontier audit report is not a score sheet. It is a document that has to survive three audiences: the lab's lawyers, who want anything commercially sensitive removed; the regulator or buyer who commissioned it, who wants findings stated plainly; and the public, if the report is published. Reconciling those three takes weeks. The report has to describe failures precisely enough to be useful and vaguely enough to avoid handing an attacker a recipe. That tension is real, and it is resolved in drafting meetings, not in the lab. Two to four weeks is a reasonable budget for a report on a straightforward evaluation. Contested findings — the evaluator found something the lab disputes — can add a month.

The third clock is the one nobody budgets for: the queue. There are not many people qualified to audit a frontier model, and the ones who are qualified are in demand. METR, Apollo Research, the UK AI Security Institute, and a handful of university groups do most of the work, and their calendars fill months ahead. A buyer who wants an audit starting next month will usually find the evaluators booked until the quarter after. The November 2026 OpenAI embedded-assessment announcement is, among other things, a capacity statement: four organizations, each running multi-month engagements, and still only a pilot. The evaluator shortage is a structural feature of the market, not a temporary bottleneck.

What the timeline does to the answer

The uncomfortable part is that the timeline changes what the audit can say. A model that takes six months to audit is a model that has been retrained, fine-tuned, or patched during those six months — and the audit describes the version that existed when testing started, not the version that ships. METR's own research makes the point from the other direction: in its January 2026 time-horizon update, it estimated that the length of tasks AI agents can complete has doubled about every 89 days since 2024. A capability that takes three months to audit is a capability that has moved by the time the report lands. The audit is a photograph of a moving target, and the longer the exposure, the blurrier the picture.

This is the honest case for shorter, narrower audits — and it is stronger than the audit industry likes to admit. A three-week embedded assessment of one system, like METR's Anthropic pilot, produces findings that are fresh, specific, and actionable, because the target is small enough to hold still. A six-month comprehensive audit produces findings that are broad, careful, and partly stale. The right choice depends on what the buyer needs: a compliance artifact that says the model was audited, or a working document that says what is wrong with it right now. The two are not the same document, and the audit that tries to be both usually ends up being neither.

There is also a counterargument the buyers should hear, because it comes from the evaluators themselves. A fast audit is not automatically a good audit, and the pressure to shorten timelines can produce the appearance of rigor without the substance. The UK AI Security Institute's transcript-analysis work — 6,390 transcripts from cybersecurity agents across nine model checkpoints — showed that pass rates on automated tests can look reassuring while the transcripts reveal refusals, tool failures, and reasoning errors the metrics never captured. Manual review of failed transcripts takes four to eight hours per evaluation cycle, and it is the minimum viable effort, not the gold standard. An audit that skips it to save a week is not faster. It is thinner.

What to plan for

A buyer commissioning a frontier audit in late 2026 should plan on four to six months from first conversation to published report, and should treat the first month as access negotiation, not testing. The testing itself will take four to eight weeks for a full evaluation, less for a narrow one. The report will take two to four weeks, longer if findings are contested. And the queue — the wait for an available evaluation team — should be measured in months, not weeks. The total is not a rounding error on the procurement timeline. It is the procurement timeline.

Three practical moves shorten it. First, scope the audit narrowly: one model, one risk class, one question. A narrow audit starts faster, finishes faster, and produces findings the lab cannot dispute as easily, because the evidence is specific. Second, negotiate access before negotiating the fee. The fee is a number; the access terms determine whether the audit can say anything worth the fee. Third, ask the evaluator for a dated report — a statement, in the document, of which model version was tested and when. An audit without a date is a certificate, not an assessment, and a certificate is what you show a regulator when you have nothing better to show.

The audit market is moving toward embedded assessment — evaluators inside the lab during training, not outside it after release — because the timeline problem is really an access problem, and embedded access solves it by making the audit continuous instead of episodic. That shift is still a pilot. Until it is the norm, the honest answer to how long an audit takes is: longer than the testing, longer than the quote, and exactly as long as the access negotiation requires. Plan for that, and the audit will arrive on time. Plan for the testing alone, and it will not.
