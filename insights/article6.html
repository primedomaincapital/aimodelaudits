How Much Does an Independent AI Model Audit Cost?

PRIME DOMAIN CAPITAL RESEARCH · 8 MIN READ · NOVEMBER 2026

In a procurement meeting in Chicago this spring, a chief information security officer put three quotes on the table. One firm promised a full independent audit of the company's customer-service model for $4,500. The second priced the same work at $28,000. The third came in above $150,000. Same model, same vendor, same three-page scope summary. The CISO's question was the right one, and it is the question this article answers: what, exactly, is being bought at each price?

The honest answer starts with an admission the industry rarely makes in public. There is no standard audit, so there is no standard price. An "AI model audit" can mean a two-week documentation review by a boutique firm, a six-week adversarial testing program by a Big Four practice, or a year-long continuous monitoring contract. The quotes on that table were not three prices for one job. They were three different jobs wearing the same name.

Four things set the price

The first is the risk tier. A model that screens job applicants or scores credit falls under the EU AI Act's high-risk category, where a conformity assessment is mandatory and the documentation burden is heavy. A frontier model with systemic risk faces a different regime again, with safety and security duties that run to adversarial testing, incident reporting, and cybersecurity proportionate to capability. A customer-service chatbot faces neither — until a tribunal decides otherwise. The fee follows the exposure, and the exposure is set by law, not by the auditor.

The second is the size and shape of what is being audited. One model with one use case is a bounded job. A platform with a dozen models, retrieval pipelines, and third-party components is a different engagement, and the price rises with every integration point. The third is depth. A documentation review checks that the paperwork exists. A full red-team program tries to break the model, and the market prices that separately: single-system red-team engagements run $16,000 to $50,000 on their own, according to published pricing from ClearAgents, a firm that lists its rates in public. The fourth is access. An auditor handed model weights, training data, and evaluation logs works faster than one negotiating API access through a vendor's legal department. Access terms, not testing, are usually what set the schedule — and the schedule sets the fee.

A price table, with names attached

Published audit prices are rare. Most firms quote behind a sales call, which is itself a useful fact: the firms that publish prices are, by definition, the ones competing on transparency. ClearAgents lists a Rapid Scan at $4,500 ($6,500 at list price) and a flagship five-dimension audit at $18,000 to $28,000, with a stated market range of $8,000 to more than $150,000. AISOFT, another firm that publishes rates, puts a typical AI traction audit at $8,000 to $15,000 over one to two weeks. Biz4Group puts AI product audit services at $5,000 to $25,000 and above, depending on scope. Primocys, a consultancy that publishes planning estimates rather than quotes, puts a focused readiness audit at $2,000 to $7,500.

Read those numbers as a map, not a menu. The $2,000 to $7,500 band buys a readiness review: architecture, data, a prioritized list of fixes. The $8,000 to $28,000 band buys a real audit of a single system, with findings a board can act on. Above $50,000, the engagement is usually enterprise-scale — multiple models, regulatory evidence packs, or frontier-model evaluation work that takes months and a team. The $150,000-plus quotes exist, and they are not a con. They are a different product, sold to buyers whose regulator can fine them up to 3% of global turnover or €15 million under the EU AI Act. For that buyer, the audit is priced against the fine, not against the work.

The second invoice

The first quote is rarely the final bill. Three costs sit outside it, and buyers who budget only for the headline number get surprised twice.

Retesting is the first. An audit that finds problems creates a second job: verifying the fixes. Some firms charge for it, some do not. ClearAgents, for example, states no retest fees within 90 days and refunds half the fee if a complete audit produces no critical or high-severity findings — a guarantee structure that tells you how confident the firm is in finding something. Ask every bidder the same two questions: what does a retest cost, and what happens if you find nothing?

Scope creep is the second. Audits expand when the auditor discovers a third-party model, an undocumented data source, or a deployment the buyer forgot to mention. Fixed prices in writing, with the scope defined before work starts, are the only defense, and they are worth negotiating harder than the price itself.

Compute and access costs are the third. Heavy evaluation runs burn API credits and GPU time, and some firms pass those through. Continuous monitoring is the recurring version of the same bill: published rates run from $4,000 a month for a single-agent program to $5,000–$20,000 a month at market rates. A buyer who commissions a one-off audit and forgets the monitoring line has bought a photograph of a moving target.

What the growth numbers actually say

The market forecasts disagree with each other so sharply that the disagreement is itself the most honest signal in the sector. SNS Insider puts third-party AI assurance services at $712.4 million in 2025, growing to $16.35 billion by 2035 at a 36.8% compound annual rate. Mordor Intelligence puts the adjacent AI audit platforms market at $3.31 billion in 2025, reaching $8.16 billion by 2031. Fact.MR forecasts AI agent audit and assurance services at $0.6 billion in 2026 and $23 billion by 2036. These cannot all be right. They do not even measure the same thing: one counts services, one counts platforms, one counts agents. A buyer who cites any single forecast as the size of "the AI audit market" is quoting a definition, not a measurement.

What the forecasts agree on is direction, and the deal flow backs them up. KPMG added AI assurance and attestation services to its AI Trust suite in September 2025, including model risk assessments and independent assurance against frameworks such as SOC and FedRAMP. Dynatrace agreed to acquire Arize AI, an AI observability firm, for $915 million in August 2026. The Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company raised $40 million in a Series A led by Ribbit Capital in September 2026 to extend its audit and insurance work from AI agents to frontier models. The Big Four are building practices, the observability vendors are buying their way in, and insurers are underwriting the risk. That is what a real market looks like — and it is also why prices will stay chaotic for a while. Every entrant is pricing a product the buyer cannot yet compare.

The case for the skeptics

The skeptics have a fair objection, and it cuts against both ends of the price range. A cheap audit can be theater: a checklist run against a model the auditor never stressed, producing a certificate that comforts a board and proves nothing. An expensive audit can be theater too. There is still no agreed standard for what an audit must contain, no accreditation body with teeth, and — as one market analysis puts it — certification depends on accredited bodies that remain scarce. A six-figure fee buys more hours, more senior people, and a thicker report. It does not buy a guarantee that the right tests were run, because nobody has yet agreed on which tests those are.

The price, in other words, is not a quality signal. It is a scope signal, a risk signal, and a signaling signal — a way for a buyer to show a regulator or a customer that it took the question seriously. That is not worthless. Procurement evidence matters, and a documented audit from a credible firm is worth more in a regulatory conversation than a perfect internal review nobody outside the company has seen. But a buyer who mistakes the price for the proof has bought the most expensive version of the same mistake.

How to buy one

Start with the risk tier, not the budget. A high-risk system under the EU AI Act needs evidence a regulator will accept; a low-risk internal tool needs a findings list an engineering team can act on. Then fix the scope in writing before anyone quotes: which models, which use cases, which data, what access. Get the retest terms in the contract, not in an email. Ask what happens if the audit finds nothing — the answer reveals whether the firm is selling findings or selling hours. And budget the monitoring line separately. A single audit is a point in time. The model will change next month, the data will drift, and the next version will need testing again.

The CISO in Chicago did the right thing in the end. She threw out the three quotes, wrote a scope document, and asked all three firms to price the same job. The spread narrowed to $18,000 against $31,000, and the difference was retesting terms and a named senior lead. That is the whole lesson. In a market with no standard product, the buyer has to write the standard herself — and then the price finally means something.
