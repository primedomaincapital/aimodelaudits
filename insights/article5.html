The Signatures That Split the Frontier: Who Signed Europe’s AI Code, and Who Didn’t

6 MIN READ · AIMODELAUDITS.COM · NOVEMBER 2026

On July 10, 2025, the European Commission received the final text of the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice: thirteen independent experts, three rounds of consultation, more than 1,600 written submissions, forty workshops. It was, on paper, a consensus document — the practical manual for complying with the EU AI Act’s model rules. Within weeks, the consensus cracked along the exact line that matters. Anthropic signed the whole thing. Meta declined. xAI signed one chapter and left the other two to be demonstrated some other way.

That split was a curiosity in 2025. On August 2, 2026, it became a risk signal with a price attached. The AI Act’s obligations for general-purpose AI providers had applied since August 2, 2025. What arrived a year later was enforcement: the Commission and its AI Office can now request documentation, run technical evaluations, demand compliance measures, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market, and fine up to 3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher. Signing the voluntary code buys no immunity. Not signing it buys a harder conversation with every customer doing diligence.

What the code actually is

The confusion starts with the document’s status. The code is not a law and not a regulation. The duties come from the AI Act itself; the code is one recognized way of meeting them. It has three chapters. Transparency applies to every provider of a general-purpose AI model: keep model documentation current, publish a summary of training content, maintain a copyright policy that honors rightsholders who opt out. Copyright sits alongside it, with the same audience. Safety and Security applies only to the most capable models — those carrying systemic risk, which the Act presumes when training a model took more than 10⁻²⁵ floating-point operations.

For that top tier, the obligations are concrete: adversarial testing, systemic risk assessment and mitigation, serious incident reporting to the AI Office, and cybersecurity proportionate to the model’s capabilities. Providers that sign the code can rely on it to show they meet Articles 53 and 55 until a harmonized standard is published. Providers that do not must demonstrate the same compliance by other means — in practice, by building their own evidence pack and defending it, model by model, to a regulator that now has the power to ask.

August 2, 2026: the teeth arrive

The enforcement calendar is the part most buyers missed. August 2, 2025: obligations apply to new models, and the code is in place. August 2, 2026: the Commission’s enforcement and penalty powers become applicable, including fines of up to 3% of global turnover or €15 million. August 2, 2027: models already on the market before August 2, 2025 must be brought into compliance. The one-year grace period was not a delay in the law. It was a delay in the consequences, and it has now expired.

The AI Office, housed in the Commission’s Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology, spent the nine months after the obligations took effect moving from consultation to active compliance reviews of frontier model providers — structured documentation demands and model evaluations, the first time any multilateral AI regulator has moved past voluntary guidance. With full Annex III high-risk system compliance arriving August 2, 2026 as well, builders deploying AI in employment screening, credit scoring, healthcare triage and biometric access control faced the same deadline from the other side of the Act.

The split, provider by provider

Anthropic signed the full code, gaining a presumption of conformity across the applicable chapters. xAI signed the Safety and Security chapter only and must demonstrate Transparency and Copyright compliance by other means. Meta declined to sign at all and must demonstrate everything by other means. None of that is a violation — the code is voluntary, and declining a chapter breaks no law. But the split is now a concrete diligence fact for anyone building on top of these models. A provider that signed has a documented, regulator-recognized route to compliance. A provider that did not has a bespoke story, and bespoke stories take longer to verify, cost more to audit, and age badly when the regulator’s questions get specific.

The threshold question sharpens the point. Based on disclosed parameter counts, published research and hardware estimates, the models presumed to breach the 10⁻²⁵ FLOPs threshold include OpenAI’s GPT-4, GPT-4o and o1, Anthropic’s Claude 3 Opus and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Google DeepMind’s Gemini 1.0 Ultra and Gemini 1.5 Pro, and xAI’s Grok 1 in its original 314-billion-parameter form. Yet training-compute figures for commercial frontier models are not publicly certified to the AI Office. The regulator presumes systemic risk from a number nobody is required to publish and nobody can independently verify — which is why the documentation regime, not the threshold, is where the real audit work lives.

California wrote its own version

The European split is not the only one. California’s Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, SB 53, signed September 29, 2025 and in force since January 1, 2026, defines a frontier developer as anyone who has trained, or begun training, a foundation model using more than 10²⁶ computing operations — a threshold ten times the EU’s. Every frontier developer must publish a transparency report when it deploys a new or substantially modified frontier model, and report critical safety incidents to the California Office of Emergency Services within 15 days of discovering them, or within 24 hours to an appropriate authority where an incident poses an imminent risk of death or serious physical injury.

Two regimes, two thresholds, two incident clocks, one practical consequence: a frontier lab now keeps two sets of books, and its customers inherit both. The labs that treat transparency reporting as a product feature — published system cards, documented evaluations, incident logs a buyer can actually read — will win procurement processes that the others lose on paperwork alone.

The case for the skeptics

The skeptics have a fair objection. A voluntary code creates no new obligations, self-assessment is not an audit, and a regulator that has never yet levied a GPAI fine may not have the technical capacity to evaluate a frontier model even when it demands access to one. The code itself is due for review by the AI Office at least once every two years, so its details will move. All true — and all beside the point for a buyer. Procurement does not wait for enforcement precedent. It asks for evidence today, and the providers who can produce it in a recognized form will be the ones whose models get deployed in regulated industries.

The audit market should read the split the same way. The work is no longer defining what an AI audit is in the abstract. It is verifying, provider by provider, whether the documentation exists, whether the evaluations were run by anyone independent, and whether the incident reporting clock would actually be met. That is a narrower job than the industry promised itself in 2024. It is also a real one, with a regulator now empowered to ask the same questions.
